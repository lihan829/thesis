\chapter{Formal Background} 
\label{chap:formal}
This section will provide mathmatical and logic basics that can be used
to represent various tonal notations and representations. Specially, I
will begin with Model Theory \citep{enderton2001mathematical}, which provide 
a framework that abstract phonological representations as mathematical 
objects, these representations can be strings, features, or ARs. This unified
representation allows us to compare representational choices
\citep{strother2017using, oakdenNotationalEquivalenceTonal2020, jardineetal20qtheoryAP}. The other purpose for this section is to lay a 
foundation for developing and understanding subsequent algorithms and learning,
some of them will be placed in separate chapters.

\section{Learning Problem}
\label{sec:formal-why}

\subsection{Representations}
What is a model exactly and why do we need it? In linguistics, all forms must be represented in 
some way, whether as a sequence of symbols, such as an orthographic form 
\textit{phonology}; in an IPA transcription, such as /fəʊ.nɑ.lə.ʤi/; or as a 
feature bundle like [+nasal, cor, ant][-sonorant, +voice]. These representations share a basic property: they consist of a finite 
set of symbols organized in a particular way. What differs across these 
representations is how and what structural dependency they implicitly 
encode. 

For example, the orthographic form \textit{phonology} 
implies that there are nine letters organized by a \textit{successor} relation: 
\textit{p} is followed by \textit{h}, which is followed by \textit{o}, 
and so on. In other words, the symbols are \textit{linearly ordered}, or
\textit{totally ordered}, which means if we assign an index to each element, the indices of any two elements can be compared.

The form /fəʊ.nɑ.lə.ʤi/ encodes additional structure. For example, the 
first syllable /fəʊ/ contains three segments, /f/, /ə/, and /ʊ/, each 
following the preceding segment in a linear order. The dot indicates 
syllabification, but its formal interpretation is open to research 
questions. It could be interpreted as a boundary symbol following /ʊ/, or 
as indicating a dominance relation: a syllable node dominates the three 
segments that belong to that syllable. It is difficult to say which 
interpretation is more appropriate, or if this representation more
advantagous than the orthographic form without specifying what relations 
are included in the representation.

A feature representation such as [+nasal, cor, ant][-sonorant, +voice] 
illustrates not only a form might have more than one type of relations, 
but the principles governing these relations may differ and even 
contridictory to one another. Phonologists know by 
convention that each bracket indicates a position of a segment; and what 
goes inside the bracket specifies the properties this segment has. In this 
case, the first bundle describes a segment has the properties of being a 
nasal, a place feature of being coronal, and more specifically, anterior; 
the second bundle describes a following segment have both properties of 
being voiced and non-sonorant at the same time. 

Note that the written order of features within a bundle does not necessarily 
encode a linguistic relation. Although [+nasal] is written before [coronal], 
and [−sonorant] before [+voice], this does not mean that one feature precedes 
or dominates the other. With respect to order, features belonging to different 
elements are comparable in the temporal sequence. However, features within the 
same bundle are simultaneous properties of a single segment, and therefore are 
unordered with respect to one another. However, this principle is not 
consistent: some phonological theories impose additional structure on 
particular features. For example, in a feature-geometric representation, a 
segment must have [coronal] specification for [anterior] to further take place. 
In such a representation, the relationship between [coronal] and [anterior] is 
therefore hierarchical rather than linear. 

These examples indicates three facts that linguistic modeling 
should be mindful of.

First, a single representation may have multiple formal interpretations, 
and it is hard to make comparison between representations if we do not
explicitly define the relations within. Second, some relations among 
symbols may be implicit and therefore not readily visible in the written 
form, such as syllabification. Conversely, the written form may appear to impose relations, such as 
linear order as in [-sonorant, +voice], but that are actually not part of 
the representation. Lastly, the principles governing a representation may 
differ, or even conflict, within the same representation, or across different 
linguistic theories.

To address these issues, we need a method that abstracts away from 
particular linguistic theories and that explicitly and unambiguously captures 
the structure of a representation and the relations among its linguistic 
units. Model theory provides one such method. Rooted in mathematical 
logic, model theory provides a formal way of describing mathematical 
objects, known as \textit{models}. A model explicitly defines a set of 
elements, called its \textit{domain}, together with one or multiple 
relations that hold among those elements. These relations collectively
define a distinct structure, and are called the \textit{signature}. We
will introduce these concepts with concrete examples in later sections.
As one will see that by using model theory, it reduces ambiguity in the 
interpretation of phonological representations by requiring their units 
and relations to be explicitly defined (Section~\ref{sec:formal_prelim} to
Section~\ref{sec:prelim.ar}). Secondly, this method provides a common 
formal foundation on which different phonological representations can be systematically compared, for example, in terms of their complexity, expressivity, or their empircal converage (see Section~\ref{sec:transduction}).

A string is a finite sequence of symbols or characters. For a 
particular language, we assume a finite set of symbols with which 
strings can be constructed. Let \(\Sigma\) denote this finite 
alphabet, and let \(\Sigma^*\) denote the set of all finite strings 
that can be formed from symbols in \(\Sigma\), including the empty 
string \(\epsilon\). For example, if a language contains only the five 
vowels \textit{a,e,i,o,u}, we may define \(\Sigma=\{a,e,i,o,u\}\), and
the possible strings defined over this alphabet can be \(aa\), \(ababa\). Similarily, for a tonal system with only two tones, H and L, 
we may define \(\Sigma=\{H,L\}.\), then \(H\), \(HL\), \(LHH\), and \(HLHL\) are all members of \(\Sigma^*\). Let $|w|$ denote the length of a string $w$. A string 
\(w\) is a substring of a string \(t\) if there exists 
two strings \(p\) and \(s\) such that \(t = pws\).
The empty string $\epsilon$ is trivially a substring of every string.

While \(\Sigma^*\) contains all possible strings that can be 
constructed from the alphabet, \(L\) contains only a subset of these 
strings (\(L \subseteq \Sigma\)). For example, some sequences, such as *bnick, 
*ptak are not ``allowed'' in English, because the sequence *bn and *pt are
phonotactic constraints in English grammar. Such grammars that consist of 
a set of constraints are known as constraint-based grammars. Formally, if 
Grammar $G$ is the set of constraints in the language, such that \(G = \{C_1, C_2, \ldots, C_n\}\), a well-formed form satisfies the \emph{conjunctive 
negative literals}: \(\lnot C_1 \land \lnot C_2 \land \ldots \land \lnot C_n\)
\citep{jardine2017LocalNature,rogers.lambert2019mol}. The learning problem is 
then to discover a grammar \(G\) that distinguishes well-formed strings from 
ill-formed ones. Let \(L(G)\) denote the set of strings permitted by \(G\). 

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:Grammarvenn}, the target language \(L\) is a subset 
of \(\Sigma^*\), the set of all possible strings over the alphabet \(\Sigma\). 
An ideal grammar would instead generate exactly the target language, such that \(G=L\). However, for most of time, different representations, learning schemes, 
or the size of data might produce different grammars. Let us consider
four possible grammars: \(G_1\), \(G_2\), \(G_3\), and \(G_4\), and none of these grammars exactly captures the target language. \(G_1\) permits only a subset of the target language and therefore \textit
{undergenerates}: it incorrectly excludes some patterns that belong to \(L\). \(G_2\) both undergenerates and overgenerates: it excludes some patterns in \(L\) while permitting some patterns outside \(L\). At the other extreme, \(G_4=\Sigma^*\), meaning that the grammar imposes no 
restrictions and permits every possible string. \(G_3\) is more restrictive 
than \(G_4\) and provides a tighter fit of the target language. Nevertheless,\(G_3\) 
still does not coincide with \(L\). Like \(G_4\), it permits some 
patterns that should be excluded and therefore overgenerates.

Figure~\ref{fig:Grammarvenn} highlights the containment or membership question 
that we need to resolve. Although this question is relatively straightforward 
for strings, it becomes more complex for tonal representations with richer 
structures. This provides a second motivation for using model theory. Explicit 
model-theoretic definitions of tonal representations provide a well-defined 
hypothesis space in which containment relations between structures and their 
factors (regardless of strings and substrings, or graphs and subgraphs) can be 
precisely specified. This hypothesis space is important for learning because it 
determines the candidate grammars that a learner can consider and how those grammars can be evaluated against the data. These topics are introduced in 
Section~\ref{sec:factors} and Section~\ref{sec:poset}.



\section{Model Theory} 

\label{sec:formal_prelim} 

\subsection{Relation}
A model is defined by a set of explicitly defined \textit{relations} which 
describe how elements of a representation are organized with regard to one 
another. All relations of a model are also colllectively referred to as the 
\textit{signature} of the model. Formally, we denote a model in the form of below:

\[
\mathcal{M} = 
\left\langle
D \mid R_1, R_2,\cdots R_n
\right\rangle.
\]


Here, \(\mathcal{M}\) is a model with which an object or a representation
can be mathmatically described. A model consists of two parts. First, 
a domain, \(D\), is a set of natural numbers that indicate how many 
elements are there in the representation. These elements can be any 
building blocks of a representation based on particular linguistic 
theories, for exmaple, IPA symbols, feature or other tonal units
such as syllable or moras. Second, a finite set of relations that are 
defined over those elements. Table~\ref{tab:relations} shows some common 
relations that will be used in this work and are also commonly seen
in other related works, even though the notation might varies. See more in 
\citet{strother2017using,oakdenNotationalEquivalenceTonal2020,chandleeBufia2019}.

\begin{table}[!ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Relation} & \textbf{Notation} & \textbf{Interpretation} \\
        \midrule
        Property   & $p(x)$            & $x$ has the property of being $p$ \\
        Successor   & $\lhd(x,y)$            & $x$ immediately precedes $y$\\
        Precedence  & $\prec(x,y)$                & $x$ precedes $y$ \\
        Dominance   & $\delta(x,y)$ & $x$ dominates $y$ \\
        Association & $\alpha(x,y)$          & $x$ is associated to $y$ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Relations commonly used in phonological representations}
    \label{tab:relations}
\end{table}


A relation can be unary or binary, depending on the number of elements it defines over. In Table~\ref{tab:relations}, the Property relation
is unary (only one element $x$ is involved), and the rest are binary 
relation (two elements, $x$ and $y$, are involved in this relation). 

The property relation can be viewed as ``assigning" a characteristic or identity to an individual element. \(p(x)\) means that the element \(x\) has the property of being \(p\). For example, in a tonal representation, \(H(x)\) means that \(x\) is an element of the domain with the property of being a high tone. \(\sigma(x)\) means that \(x\) is an element of the domain with the property of being a syllable.

A relation may also be binary and specifies a connection between two elements. For example, a successor relation specifies that one element \(x\) immediately is followed by another element \(y\). In Section~\ref{sec:formal-why}, we saw that the orthographic form \textit{phonology} follows this pattern: each letter
follows the previous letter. Formally, for the first three letters: \textit{pho}, there are three positions and we index them as \(x, y, z\). The unary relations are: \(p(x)\), \(h(y)\), and \(o(z)\). This means x is p, y is h, and z is o, and \(p\) is immediately followed by \(h\), and \(h\) is immediately followed by \(o\). Using \(\lhd\) to denote the successor relation, these relations can be written as \(\lhd(x,y)\) and \(\lhd(y,z)\).

A precedence relation resembles successor but does not require adjacency; it 
specifies only the relative order between two elements. Since \(p\) precedes 
\(h\), \(h\) precedes \(o\), so that \(p\) also precedes \(o\). Given, \(p(x)\),
\(h(y)\), and \(o(z)\), the precedence relations are \(\prec(x,y)\), \(\prec(y, z)\), 
and also \(\prec(x,z)\). In contrast, \(\lhd(x,z)\) does not hold since they 
are not adjacent.

If successor and precedence relations are typically represented horizontally, 
dominance and association relations are often represented vertically. They 
connect elements across different tiers or levels of structure. A dominance 
relation specifies a hierarchical relation between elements. One example of such
relation is seen in metrical theory where a syllable can dominates the 
onset: \(\delta(\sigma, o)\). Such relation is one-directional: an onset does not dominate a syllable. Association relation specifies a connection between elements on different tiers, and unlike dominance, it is bi-directional, or
associative. In Autosegmental phonology, a syllable is associated with H tone 
also suggests a H tone is associated with a syllable. In other words, \(\alpha(\sigma, H) \Rightarrow \alpha(H, \sigma)\).

From here on, I will begin by showing how segmental sequences and feature
bundles are represented model-theoretically, and then move on to metrical 
and autosegmental representations. Segmental and feature-based representations can largely 
be modeled as one-dimensional structures, in which elements are organized 
primarily by one axis of a linear order. Metrical and autosegmental 
representations, by contrast, require richer relational structures are more 
naturally modeled as graphs, where multiple tiers are involved and elements 
on different tiers are bounded by dominance or association relations.


\subsection{String Model}
\label{sec:prelim.string}
The most basic way to represent tones is to reprense thtem as
strings. For a disyllabic word \textit{tá.tǎ}, if we abstract 
away the segmental informatin, and only keep its tones, we will 
get a sequence of \textit{H.R}: the first syllable has a H tone, 
and the second tone is R. 

Let us assume an alphabet including the four basic tonal units and also 
the symbol for the syllable boundary \(\Sigma = \{., H, L, R, F\}\). 
A visulization of this model to represent \textit{H.R} and its signature 
are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:model:str}. In its signature,
$D = \{1, 2, 3\}$ indicates that there are three elements in the string.
Each element has a linguistically meaningful label to reflect its  
property.  Position $\{1\}$ is labelled with \textbf{H}, $\{2\}$ with  
\textbf{.}, and $\{3\}$ with \textbf{R}.

by $\lhd$.  For example, $\lhd = \{\langle 1, 2\rangle, \langle 2,
3\rangle, \langle 3, 4\rangle\}$ specifies that position 1 (element labelled with H) immediately precedes position 2 (labelled with $.$) which
immediately precedes position 3 (labelled with R).

\subsection{Feature Representations}
\label{sec:prelim.feature}


The feature-based representation also arranges symbols along a single axis.
Like the string model, the signature of the feature model
includes single binary successor relation $\rightarrow$ defining linear order.
Unlike the string models, there are five unary relations:
\{+Low, -Low, +Contour, -Contour, Boundary\},
where \texttt{low} and \texttt{contour} are binary features, 
and \texttt{Boundary} is treated as a privative feature. (Note in this representation \texttt{[$\pm$Contour]} is only used to indicate monomoraic contour tones.)
In the string model, each position is true of exactly one unary relation.
However, in the feature model, positions may be true of more 
than one unary relation.
The position 1 and 4 have the feature \texttt{+Low} and \texttt{-Contour}.
\citet{strother2017using} and \citet{vu2018statistical} refer to such 
models as \textit{unconventional} because they are not the standard 
way to model strings in theoretical computer science (though it is 
common in phonology).

\subsection{Autosegmental Representation}
\label{sec:prelim.ar}

Unlike the string model, where elements are ordered linearly along a
single axis, an AR distributes linguistic information
across multiple tiers. In this work, in addition to the basic tone and
syllable tiers, we incorporate a mora tier, 
and treat the mora as a secondary unit subordinated

\section{Factors and Subfactors}
\label{sec:factors}

The previous sections have looked at various structures that can be used to represent tonal information: they can be strings, or relational structures, or graphs. From here, we will abstract
away from specific presentation of the structures:
and consider them as \textit{factors}, which are connected relational structures. 

\subsection{Connectedness}
\citet{chandleeBufia2019} define a factor as a connected relational structure, and a subfactor as a connected substructure of a factor.
The definition of connectedness (stands between two elements) is given in Definition~\ref{def.connected} and a connected structure is defined in Definition~\ref{def.connect.struc}.

\begin{definition}[Connectedness]
$ C := \{
    (x,y) \in D \times D \mid  \exists i \in \{1 \cdots n\},
    \exists (x_1 \cdots x_m) \in R_i,
    \exists s, t \in \{1 \cdots m\},
    x = x_s, y = x_t
\}$
\label{def.connected}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Connected Structure]
    \label{def.connect.struc}
A structure $\mathcal{S} = \langle D; R_1, R_2, \ldots, R_n \rangle$ is
\emph{connected} if and only if
\(
    \forall x,y \in D,\quad (x,y) \in C^{*}.
\)
\end{definition}


Definition~\ref{def.connected} states that two elements $x$ and $y$ in 
the domain $D$ are connected, if there exsits an \(R_i\) whose relational 
tuple contains both \(x\) and \(y\). Informally, if there exists a path 
connecting from element \(x\) to element \(y\) through any relation in 
the model, then \(x\) and \(y\) are connected. Definition~\ref{def.connect.struc} 
states that a structure is connected if every pair of elements are in 
the symmetric transitive closure of C , denoted as \(C^{*}\).

However, this connectedness restriction need not be imposed globally for ARs.
Since ARs allow unassociated elements across tiers. For example, the ARs
that have two tiers with no association lines between them, are not connected in the sense of \citet
{chandleeBufia2019}; but they are still valid ARs, or at least valid as an intermiediate 
representation of a tonal structure. For this reason, the connectness for ARs is a
weaker condition as \emph{tier-wise connectedness}: an AR is a valid connected structure
if elements are in connectedess within the tier.

\subsection{Containment}
An important concept when considering relational
structures is \textit{containment}. 
Informally, one structure in contained in another one if it can be
found as a subpart of the other. For strings, substrings are a 
common example of containment; for graphs, subgraphs. 
In model theory, what the commonly used term ``substructure" refers to is
more precisely known as ``subfactor", and it makes distinction between the two concepts \citep{rogers.lambert2019mol,chandleeBufia2019}.






Let us consider the simplest and trivial case of subfactors, which is when two factors are identical, or formally, they are \textit{isomorphic}. 

\begin{definition}[Isomorphism]
Let
$\mathcal{A} =
\langle D; R_1^{\mathcal{A}},\ldots,R_n^{\mathcal{A}}\rangle$
and
$
\mathcal{B} =
\langle D; R_1^{\mathcal{B}},\ldots,R_n^{\mathcal{B}}\rangle
$
be two relational structures over the same signature.
$\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ are \emph{isomorphic}, written
$\mathcal{A} \cong \mathcal{B}$, if there exists a bijection
$f:A \to B$ such that for every $m$-ary relation symbol $R_i$ and all
$a_1,\ldots,a_m \in A$,
\[
R_i^{\mathcal{A}}(a_1,\ldots,a_m)
\Longleftrightarrow
R_i^{\mathcal{B}}(f(a_1),\ldots,f(a_m)).
\]
\end{definition}

Informally, the two factors are identical in terms of their structure 
and properties. An example of two isomorphic factors is shown in 
Figure~\ref{fig:isomorphic}, where all nodes and edges are identical,
except the index (which is not in the signature) of the nodes are different.

A more complex case arises when two factors are not isomorphic, but one is
\emph{homomorphic} to the other. A homomorphism does not require the two
structures to be identical. Rather, it requires that all relations holding in
the smaller factor are preserved under a bijective function into the larger structure.
In other words, if two elements stand in a relation \(R\) the smaller factor,
then their images under the function must stand in the corresponding relation
in the larger factor. \citet{chandleeBufia2019} define this relation through
\textit{restriction} and \textit{containment}: a factor is contained in another
factor if there exists a restriction of the larger factor and a function from
the smaller factor to this restriction such that all relations in the smaller
factor are preserved in the larger factor.

\begin{definition}
	\label{def.res}
	\textit{ $A= \langle D^A; R^A_1, \ldots, R^A_n \rangle$ is a \emph{restriction}
		of $B
		= \langle D^B; R^B_1, \ldots, R^B_n \rangle$ iff $D^A \subseteq D^B$ and for
		each $m$-ary relation $R^A_i$, we have $R^A_i = \{(x_1, \ldots, x_m) \in R^B_i \mid x_1, \ldots, x_m \in D^A\}$. }
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
	\label{def.contain}
	\textit{Structure $A$ is \emph{contained} in structure $B$
		($A \sqsubseteq B$) if there exists a restriction of $B$, denoted
		$B'$, and there exists a function $h:A \rightarrow B'$ such that for
		each $m$-ary relation $R_i$ in the model signature:
		\(R_i(a_1, \ldots, a_m) \in A \implies R(h(a_1), \ldots, h(a_m)) \in B'.\)}
\end{definition}

\section{Partically Ordered Space}
\label{sec:poset}
The containment relation is particularly important because it provides
a natural \textit{ordering} over the space of all logically possible 
structures. The ordering is not total, like a linear order; it is a 
\textit{partial} ordering. Below gives the formal definition of total and partial ordering \citep{CLRS2022}.

\begin{definition}[Total Ordering]
A \emph{total ordering} on a set $D$ is a binary relation
$\leq \,\subseteq D \times D$ satisfying the following properties
for all $x,y,z \in D$:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1em, parsep=-1em]
    \item Reflexivity: $x \leq x$.
    \item Antisymmetry: If $x \leq y$ and $y \leq x$,
    then $x = y$.
    \item Transitivity: If $x \leq y$ and $y \leq z$,
    then $x \leq z$.
    \item Totality: $x \leq y$ or $y \leq x$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Partial Ordering]
A \emph{partial ordering} on a set $D$ is a binary relation
$\leq \,\subseteq D \times D$ that is reflexive, antisymmetric,
and transitive. 
\end{definition}

Unlike a total ordering, a partial ordering
allows two distinct elements to be incomparable: neither
$x \leq y$ nor $y \leq x$ need hold. In other words, in a partially-ordered space, some elements can be comparable, but others are not.

To illustrate, Figure~\ref{fig:hasse.str} displays a
non-exhaustive portion of the partial ordering of possible strings
over the alphabet $\Sigma = \{a,b\}$ in terms of containment relation. 

At the bottom of the ordering is
the empty string $\epsilon$ (with the length of \(0\)) and the more complex strings occur
higher in the space (with increasing length \(k\) value). Whenever two elements are connected by a
line, it means that the higher element \textit{contains} the lower element;
hence they are comparable.
Otherwise, the two elements are incomparable and neither contains the other.
For example, no line relates the \(a\) to \(b\), because a doesn't contain b or vice versa. However, a line is drawn from \(a\) to \(aa, ab\) and
\(ba\), because \(a\) is contained each of them. As
defined above, \(aa, ab, ba\) and \(aaa\), and any other strings that
contain \(a\) are superfactors of \(a\); correspondingly, \(a\) is a
subfactor of each of these strings.

There can be many layers between a superfactor and one of its 
subfactors. When there are no structures ordered 
between a superfactor and one of its subfactors, the 
superfactor is called a \textit{next superfactor}. 
In this sense, among all superfactors of \(a\) such as 
\(\{aa, ab, ba, aaa, aab, \cdots\}\), only those superfactors that 
are exactly one level above are next superfactors of \(a\), 
namely \(\{aa, ab, ba\}\).

The partial ordering provides an important guide for well-formedness inference \citep{Yolyan2025,chandleeBufia2019,rawski2021structure}. The well-formedness is entailed downwards: if a factor is considered
well-formed, or is attested in the data; all its subfactors are also well-formed. For instance, if \textit{aba} is a wellformed factor, all of its subfactors, that is, \textit{ab, ba, a, b} and trivially \(\epsilon\) 
are also wellformed. On the contrary, the 
illformedness passes upwards:
if a factor is ill-formed, then all its
superfactors that contains this ill-formed factor are also ill-formed. For example, if \textit{ab} is an illformed structure, then
all factors containing it, \textit{aab,aba,abb...} are all illformed. However, \textit{ab} being illformed does not provide any guidence of the wellformedness of \textit{ba}, since the two are incomparable.

This example demonstrates a hypothesis space of strings. In Chapter~\ref{chap:tones}, I will show how such space can be constructed in terms of tonal patterns, which is more complex.


\section {Transduction}
\label{sec:transduction}

The previous section introduced model theory as a unified framework for 
defining phonological forms proposed by different theories. It essentially 
express how the consisting units within a representation relate to one 
another.

This section uses the same method and moves beyond an individual 
phonological form to the mapping between representations, that
is how one representation can transform to another one. This 
question is related to two questions in tonal phonology. One is the UR-SR 
learning over model-theoretic representation. Given two representations at different 
abstract levels, how does one derive from one to another. Unlike segmental
phonology where many processes can be written as SPE-style rules,
in tonal phonology, the transformation between UR to SR is largely
generalized by delinking and modifying the association lines of ARs.
These generalizations are what the linear rules struggle to capture.
The second is representation conversion: 
how representations grounded  in different linguistic theories relate to one another. 
Logical transduction can be used to address these questions, which is to 
view two models as the input and output of a function. One example is  
\citet{oakdenNotationalEquivalenceTonal2020} argues that two feature-geometric 
models proposed by \citet{bao1999structure} and \citet{yip1989contour} 
might appear to be different visually, but they actually do not differ in 
their empirical coverage. The two models can be converted into each other
using a set of logical formulas, hence they are ``notationally equivalent''.


This section will give a general introduction to logical transduction using
the representation conversion as an example, using the examples from
\citet{oakdenNotationalEquivalenceTonal2020}. As for the UR-SR mapping, it 
will be discussed in the Chapter~\ref{chap:process}, with a focus on the 
delinking and modifying association lines in ARs.

For example, \citet{yip1989contour} proposes that a contour can be either decomposed 
into two sequences of level tones and the other one bahave as a single tone. 
The distinction is how the tones associate with the tonal node.

To transform a branching-node model (input) into a single-node 
model (output), we can compare their signatures and observe three 
changes: (i) the domain D is reduced (from 5 to 4); (ii) the labels
L and H remain unchanged; and (iii) the other relations are modified.

For each relation or domian change, we need to define a formula 
$\phi_{R_i}$ such that the interpretation of $R$ in the output is 
derived from its interpretation in the input model.
Specifically:

\begin{itemize}
    

\item \(
\phi_{\textsf{Domain}} := \text{True}.
\)
This formula leaves the output domain unrestricted.

\item \(
\textsc{Copy} := 1.
\)
This means that no additional copies of input elements are introduced in the output structure.
\item 
\(
\phi_{\textsf{license}}(x) :=
R_\circ(x) \wedge
\neg \exists z \bigl(R_\lhd(z,x) \wedge R_\circ(z)\bigr).
\)
This formula licenses only the first tonal node. Unlicensed tonal nodes disappear together with relations associated with them.

\item \(
\phi_{R_H}(x) := R_H(x).
\)
This preserves the H-tone relation: elements that belong to $R_H$ in the input
also belong to $R_H$ in the output.

\item \(
\phi_{R_L}(x) := R_L(x).
\)
This preserves the L-tone relation: elements that belong to $R_L$ in the input
also belong to $R_L$ in the output.

\item \(
\phi_{R_\circ}(x) :=
R_\circ(x) \wedge
\neg \exists z \bigl(R_\lhd(z,x) \wedge R_\circ(z)\bigr).
\)
This keeps only the first tonal node in the output. \todo{is it accurate?}

\item \(
\phi_{R_\sigma}(x) := R_\sigma(x).
\)
This preserves the syllable relation: elements that belong to $R_\sigma$ in the
input also belong to $R_\sigma$ in the output. 

\item \(
\phi_{R_\lhd}(x,y) := R_\lhd(x,y).
\)
This preserves precedence: if pair $(x,y)$ that belong to $R_\lhd$ in the input
also belong to $R_\lhd$ in the output. Or if \(x\) follows \(y\) in the input, it does so as well in the output.

\item \(
\phi_{R_\alpha}(x,y) :=
\colorbox{gray!20}{$\displaystyle R_\alpha(x,y)$}
\vee
\colorbox{gray!40}{$\displaystyle
\left(
    \bigl(R_H(x) \vee R_L(x)\bigr)
    \wedge R_\circ(y)
\right)
$}
\vee
\colorbox{gray!60}{$\displaystyle
\bigl(
    R_\circ(x)
    \wedge R_\sigma(y)
\bigr)$}
\)
This redefines association based on three cases (corresponding to three shaded conditions). A pair $(x,y)$ belongs to $R_\alpha$ in the output if 
\begin{itemize}
    \item $(x,y)$ belongs to $R_\alpha$ in the input; or
    \item $x$ is a tone (H or L) and $y$ is a tonal node (\(\circ\))
    \item $x$ is a tonal node (\(\circ\))and
$y$ is a syllable  (\(\sigma\)).
\end{itemize}


\end{itemize}
This set of formulas first faithfully preserves the domain, the L and H tone nodes, and the syllable nodes, but modifies the $\alpha$-relations: tones are associated with tonal nodes, and tonal nodes are associated with syllables. The merging of tonal nodes is handled by licensing only the first tonal node. As a result, all unlicensed tonal nodes, together with any relations associated with them, are ignored in the output.
